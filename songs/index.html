Song One

Time Without War

Samba Pagode

On content

This song was written as an homage to Jorge Aragão, Pericles, and other 90s Pagode GOATs. Inspired by favorites like Lucidez and Eu e Você Sempre, my goal was to slow the world down and deliver a love song that tells two stories. How can we talk about love through the lens of war? Em and I focused on using imagery of war and juxtaposing that against images of love, to show what the main force of the show is about. This is the song that comes up the most in the show (4 times), and it kept feeling relevant, no matter the context of the moment.

On genre

Samba Pagode as we know it today wouldn't have been invented yet, but the traces of it would have existed. Pagode is one of those words that's almost impossible to define in any specific way, but that hasn't stopped many academics and musicologists over the years. It's arguably the biggest form of Brazilian music popular in Brazil — but it's hard to say what that means, exactly. In Philip Galinsky's Co-option, Cultural Resistance, and Afro-Brazilian Identity: A History of the Pagode Samba Movement in Rio de Janeiro, he redefines pagode to refer to several specific genres of Samba — all with many similarities, but also rife with a great level of differences. Afro-Brazilian music historian Nei Lopes talks about the origins of the term itself in an unpublished manuscript of his, saying that it comes from the word Pagoda, meaning an asian temple, and somehow it came to mean a kind of "merrymaking," or "spree." This would later signify just any kind of joyous event in a public or semi-public space, where musicians would get together to play samba of some kind.

There's an argument that samba has always been pagode, since the dawn of the genre itself, but it's hard to pinpoint where exactly the term shifts. Nevertheless, something that looked and felt like pagode was forming, in some way or another, at the end of the 20th and beginning of the 21st century, with these events across the cities in parties and gatherings in black communities in Rio. The festive, communal gatherings at the homes of legendary figures like Pixinguinha and Tia Ciata in the early 20th century were, in essence, the original pagodes, and this is where the birth of samba originates.

On orchestration & harmony

I focused on the rule-breaking minor V, borrowing from Eu e Você Sempre, which is interchangeable with the bVII in the chorus, both of which lead to the IV plagally. This creates the narrative of heartbreak that the I chord has to go through because its V chord becomes minor, thus setting up the beauty of the IV chord, only to break our hearts with the minor IV. The second line of the A section borrows from the other Aragão, Lucidez, utilizing the II7 superimposed on top of the I to create the sense of wonder and rising intrigue. I always feel like that #11 makes you raise your eyebrows a bit, and that moment feels right for that. We're using simple instruments here — the guitar, the piano, the pandeiro, some shakers — but we get a sound that's extremely full.

Song Two

They Hum

Samba Chula · Samba de Roda

On content

This piece was very exciting to place into the show, and I knew exactly where it would go. We considered the idea of the list song as a pinnacle of Brazilian music, thinking about songs like Águas de Março, a long-time favorite, and more contemporary utilizations of the list in Brazilian music like Caetano Veloso's ode to his language, Língua. It comes up right before our three naval characters get off the boat and join the others at the bar, and we thought it would be humorous to have an in-world song that they love about listing things coming off of a boat. The key to the whole song lies in the captain's verse: "Stomp and haul and shout all the / Delicate things sailors write songs about." We're singing a song about the things that sailors write songs about.

On genre

This is a samba chula, which is part of the wider Bahian tradition of Samba de Roda. Samba de Roda (not to be confused with Roda de Samba, which just means "samba circle") is often considered one of the earliest or most foundational forms of samba, laying the cultural groundwork for many of the future forms. Created by enslaved Africans and their descendants, the genre embeds Afro-Brazilian religious elements and expands beyond just the music itself, moving towards dance, poetry, lyricism, and more. Samba chula, from Recôncavo in Bahia, is one of the oldest forms of Samba de Roda, and was born from the work of enslaved people on sugarcane farms. To quote a chula researcher: "In this environment of encounter between cultures, the mixture took place: the African drumming, the Arab tambourine, the Portuguese viola, the musical sensibility of the Sudanese and the singing of lament became samba chula, one of the symbols of the Recôncavo since the 19th century."

On orchestration & harmony

The harmony here is pretty simple. I studied samba chula with Roberto Mendes, one of the few on the forefront of preservation and appreciation of the style, and he speaks about it not as folklore to be preserved under glass, but as a living classical tradition. He describes the songwriting as necessitating "perfect verses," beautiful poetry carried by simply two chords. The verses oscillate between G and C, with the signature dead-note plucking style of chula guitar — heavily rhythmic, and repetitive. It's accompanied by the cavaquinho, a four-stringed Portuguese-turned-Brazilian instrument taking the place of the ten-stringed Portuguese-turned-Brazilian instrument, the viola machete, playing syncopated arpeggiations of the chords. It's also accompanied by percussion, most of which is customary in any samba, but the key instrument to highlight is the prato-e-faca, or "plate and knife," coming from a long history of repurposing household items as percussion when drums weren't available.

Song Three

Between the Players

Samba de Raiz

On content

This was the first song I wrote for the show, with Eva Saenz and Rabiah Kabir's original verses (in Spanish!), all the way back last spring quarter, for the final of my class called Black Brazil, taught by Diana Klinger. The verses were entirely different, and it took quite a while, and a lot of reworking, to get the verses to a place where I felt like they were as strong as the chorus was. A lot of samba is about… samba. I'm thinking about songs like Paulinho da Viola's Argumento, which just states, "all we want to hear is the pandeiro, the cavaquinho and the tamborim," or Grupo Fundo de Quintal's A Batucada de Nossos Tantãs, which just talks about how "everyone is always trying to silence the batucada of our tantãs." We tried to embed this metasamba idea of singing about the band that is in the room before us, and I think that was successful. It's so enjoyable to hear "everyone knows how to sing the chorus," sung by a large group of people who, indeed, know the chorus.

On genre

This pulls from samba de raiz, and other traditional forms of the genre, to create a really pure and fun song that could be sung in the roda, the circle. A large inspiration is Adoniran Barbosa's Trem das Onze, and the very idea of the "lai lai lai" comes from a long history of vocables in samba — especially from songs that would get stuck in my head when sitting on a street corner in Rio, not understanding the words yet, but knowing I'd heard that one before. I wanted to give the audience that feeling when we come back to it at the very end of the show.

On orchestration & harmony

This is fun. We finally get to the true samba instrumentation: violão sete-cordas (seven-string guitar), cavaquinho, pandeiro, tamborim, agogô, ganzá, surdo, rebolo, and more. This batucada is what makes the engine of this song move forward, and we get a glimpse of that when we reprise it at the end, when Bruno leads the group in a percussion call-and-response. This is the heartbeat of the show, and it's the song I think people will be walking out singing. Harmonically and melodically, I was aiming to capture the dual nature of this kind of music: we have a driving, minor-focused refrain section, and a lighter, sweet-sounding verse. The verse also borrows melodic ideas from Almir Guineto's Conselho, with its jumps and stretching over the top of the range of where you thought the melody was going to go.
