Song One

Time Without War

Samba Pagode

On content

This song was written as an homage to Jorge Aragão, Pericles, and other 90s Pagode GOATs. Inspired by favorites like Lucidez and Eu e Você Sempre, my goal was to slow the world down and deliver a love song that tells two stories. How can we talk about love through the lens of war? Em and I focused on using imagery of war and juxtaposing that against images of love, to show what the main force of the show is about. This is the song that comes up the most in the show (4 times), and it kept feeling relevant, no matter the context of the moment.

On genre

Samba Pagode as we know it today wouldn't have been invented yet, but the traces of it would have existed. Pagode is one of those words that's almost impossible to define in any specific way, but that hasn't stopped many academics and musicologists over the years. It's arguably the biggest form of Brazilian music popular in Brazil — but it's hard to say what that means, exactly. In Philip Galinsky's Co-option, Cultural Resistance, and Afro-Brazilian Identity: A History of the Pagode Samba Movement in Rio de Janeiro, he redefines pagode to refer to several specific genres of Samba — all with many similarities, but also rife with a great level of differences. Afro-Brazilian music historian Nei Lopes talks about the origins of the term itself in an unpublished manuscript of his, saying that it comes from the word Pagoda, meaning an asian temple, and somehow it came to mean a kind of "merrymaking," or "spree." This would later signify just any kind of joyous event in a public or semi-public space, where musicians would get together to play samba of some kind.

There's an argument that samba has always been pagode, since the dawn of the genre itself, but it's hard to pinpoint where exactly the term shifts. Nevertheless, something that looked and felt like pagode was forming, in some way or another, at the end of the 20th and beginning of the 21st century, with these events across the cities in parties and gatherings in black communities in Rio. The festive, communal gatherings at the homes of legendary figures like Pixinguinha and Tia Ciata in the early 20th century were, in essence, the original pagodes, and this is where the birth of samba originates.

On orchestration & harmony

I focused on the rule-breaking minor V, borrowing from Eu e Você Sempre, which is interchangeable with the bVII in the chorus, both of which lead to the IV plagally. This creates the narrative of heartbreak that the I chord has to go through because its V chord becomes minor, thus setting up the beauty of the IV chord, only to break our hearts with the minor IV. The second line of the A section borrows from the other Aragão, Lucidez, utilizing the II7 superimposed on top of the I to create the sense of wonder and rising intrigue. I always feel like that #11 makes you raise your eyebrows a bit, and that moment feels right for that. We're using simple instruments here — the guitar, the piano, the pandeiro, some shakers — but we get a sound that's extremely full.

Song Two

They Hum

Samba Chula · Samba de Roda

On content

This piece was very exciting to place into the show, and I knew exactly where it would go. We considered the idea of the list song as a pinnacle of Brazilian music, thinking about songs like Águas de Março, a long-time favorite, and more contemporary utilizations of the list in Brazilian music like Caetano Veloso's ode to his language, Língua. It comes up right before our three naval characters get off the boat and join the others at the bar, and we thought it would be humorous to have an in-world song that they love about listing things coming off of a boat. The key to the whole song lies in the captain's verse: "Stomp and haul and shout all the / Delicate things sailors write songs about." We're singing a song about the things that sailors write songs about.

On genre

This is a samba chula, which is part of the wider Bahian tradition of Samba de Roda. Samba de Roda (not to be confused with Roda de Samba, which just means "samba circle") is often considered one of the earliest or most foundational forms of samba, laying the cultural groundwork for many of the future forms. Created by enslaved Africans and their descendants, the genre embeds Afro-Brazilian religious elements and expands beyond just the music itself, moving towards dance, poetry, lyricism, and more. Samba chula, from Recôncavo in Bahia, is one of the oldest forms of Samba de Roda, and was born from the work of enslaved people on sugarcane farms. To quote a chula researcher: "In this environment of encounter between cultures, the mixture took place: the African drumming, the Arab tambourine, the Portuguese viola, the musical sensibility of the Sudanese and the singing of lament became samba chula, one of the symbols of the Recôncavo since the 19th century."

On orchestration & harmony

The harmony here is pretty simple. I studied samba chula with Roberto Mendes, one of the few on the forefront of preservation and appreciation of the style, and he speaks about it not as folklore to be preserved under glass, but as a living classical tradition. He describes the songwriting as necessitating "perfect verses," beautiful poetry carried by simply two chords. The verses oscillate between G and C, with the signature dead-note plucking style of chula guitar — heavily rhythmic, and repetitive. It's accompanied by the cavaquinho, a four-stringed Portuguese-turned-Brazilian instrument taking the place of the ten-stringed Portuguese-turned-Brazilian instrument, the viola machete, playing syncopated arpeggiations of the chords. It's also accompanied by percussion, most of which is customary in any samba, but the key instrument to highlight is the prato-e-faca, or "plate and knife," coming from a long history of repurposing household items as percussion when drums weren't available.

Song Three

Between the Players

Samba de Raiz

On content

This was the first song I wrote for the show, with Eva Saenz and Rabiah Kabir's original verses (in Spanish!), all the way back last spring quarter, for the final of my class called Black Brazil, taught by Diana Klinger. The verses were entirely different, and it took quite a while, and a lot of reworking, to get the verses to a place where I felt like they were as strong as the chorus was. A lot of samba is about… samba. I'm thinking about songs like Paulinho da Viola's Argumento, which just states, "all we want to hear is the pandeiro, the cavaquinho and the tamborim," or Grupo Fundo de Quintal's A Batucada de Nossos Tantãs, which just talks about how "everyone is always trying to silence the batucada of our tantãs." We tried to embed this metasamba idea of singing about the band that is in the room before us, and I think that was successful. It's so enjoyable to hear "everyone knows how to sing the chorus," sung by a large group of people who, indeed, know the chorus.

On genre

This pulls from samba de raiz, and other traditional forms of the genre, to create a really pure and fun song that could be sung in the roda, the circle. A large inspiration is Adoniran Barbosa's Trem das Onze, and the very idea of the "lai lai lai" comes from a long history of vocables in samba — especially from songs that would get stuck in my head when sitting on a street corner in Rio, not understanding the words yet, but knowing I'd heard that one before. I wanted to give the audience that feeling when we come back to it at the very end of the show.

On orchestration & harmony

This is fun. We finally get to the true samba instrumentation: violão sete-cordas (seven-string guitar), cavaquinho, pandeiro, tamborim, agogô, ganzá, surdo, rebolo, and more. This batucada is what makes the engine of this song move forward, and we get a glimpse of that when we reprise it at the end, when Bruno leads the group in a percussion call-and-response. This is the heartbeat of the show, and it's the song I think people will be walking out singing. Harmonically and melodically, I was aiming to capture the dual nature of this kind of music: we have a driving, minor-focused refrain section, and a lighter, sweet-sounding verse. The verse also borrows melodic ideas from Almir Guineto's Conselho, with its jumps and stretching over the top of the range of where you thought the melody was going to go.

A note from Emily

Sebastian wanted the songs of this world to feel like they already exist as crowd favorites. Singing about singing is one such classic in samba as well as folk traditions worldwide. In a mix of voices, it's possible to hear familiar hints of someone you're listening for. This song is about the phenomenon of catching echoes of people we've lost in songs they might have sung.

Song Four

The Storm / Der Mabul

Forró · Xaxado · Klezmer

On content

This is another list song, and this time we're listing the things that are found in a flood. We knew this song would happen when a storm came, and the only thing that made sense was to do an upbeat, high-energy song about the water flooding a boat. A lot of the music was supposed to conceptually relate to what is happening literally, but not be overtly literal. In this case, that distinction is clear. The lyrics do an interesting thing: when the three parts are overlapped, they intertwine the meanings in clever ways. Kudos to Emily for all of that.

For the second section of the piece, we move to Yiddish. The Yiddish lyrics — expertly crafted by Yiddish comparative literature scholar Elizabeth Shepherd — tell a similar story to the first part, but instead use the word מבול for "flood," borrowing from the biblical flood and giving it a grandiosity that is well reflected in the beautiful language.

On genre

We're moving northeast with this one. This play is inspired in large part by the Northeast of Brazil, so it felt only appropriate that this song become part of that identity. The song takes inspiration from forró, a hugely influential musical tradition from northeastern Brazil. Someone once described it to me as "bandit music." There is a real historical connection here. This piece is especially inspired by xaxado, a style of music and dance associated with nomadic bandits, or cangaceiros, who used it to commemorate victories in battle. Over time, it became part of the broader traditional culture of northeastern Brazil. The name xaxado is said to come from the sound of sandals scraping against the ground as people danced.

Rhythmically, this music is often built around the baião pattern: a three-pulse structure with two dotted eighth notes followed by a single eighth note. That rhythm shows up in forró and in many related northeastern Brazilian styles. Forró itself draws from many rural rhythms across the Northeast. Its etymology is also interesting. One possible origin — maybe apocryphal, but I like it — is the word forrobodó, which people use to describe a party or commotion that seems to go on forever, which feels pretty close to the spirit of this show.

As the song continues, our dentist, Leon, starts to realize that these rhythms remind him of another musical tradition he knows well: klezmer. Klezmer is Jewish traditional music from Central and Eastern Europe, often played at weddings and celebrations. It combines Jewish religious and Middle Eastern sounds with Slavic, Ottoman, and Romanian folk influences, creating music that is deeply danceable and full of joy, ache, and ornament.

There are also some surprising sonic overlaps between klezmer and northeastern Brazilian music. Both traditions make prominent use of instruments like accordion and clarinet, and both often include tambourine-like percussion. In Brazilian music, that might be the pandeiro; in klezmer and nearby Middle Eastern traditions, it might be the riq. I decided to bring these worlds together because they have always sounded connected to me. I imagine Leon would hear that connection too — and that this rhythm might remind him of a song his grandfather once sang to him.

On orchestration & harmony

I used the common forró instrumentation, making some alterations. I was very excited to buy Reid an accordion, and learn about the methods of playing baião on it — using different ways of accelerating the bellows, and accentuating notes with the bass keys and the treble keys syncopated against each other. The other instruments are triángulo — a triangle hand-made by Bola, our instrument dealer — along with the pandeiros and more, the surdo (replacing the zabumba, the forró bass drum), as well as the violin and cello, making their first appearances, playing the baião rhythms as well.

The harmony starts pretty tame, V–i, in Am then C major (echoing the "Between the Players" relativity switch), but when it goes to the klezmer, we move into Freygish territory, or "Phrygian dominant" — also called "hijaz," a common mode in Jewish and Arab music.

Song Five

The Green Ray

Samba-Canção

On content

This song began with a story my mother used to tell me about the green flash: the brief green light that can appear just as the sun slips below the horizon. That image had already surfaced a few times in the play, and I wanted to finally give it its own musical moment.

Emily's lyrics do a beautiful job of capturing the sky at that exact threshold: orange clouds, pink and peach, the last flash of light before the day disappears. There is something naturally theatrical about the image. Everyone is looking in the same direction, waiting for something that may or may not happen, and then, for one second, the world seems to reveal itself.

On genre

This is a samba-canção, a slower and more contemplative kind of samba. I knew I needed one of these in the show. These softer, more inward songs have always had a special place in my heart: the kind of song that lets harmony, color, and breath do the work.

The inspirations go beyond samba as well. I was thinking about the intimacy of Rita Payés' Quién lo diría, the floating lyricism of Luiz Murá's Águila Andina, and the late-night ache of Chico Buarque's Samba e Amor. Those songs all live in a space where softness carries a lot of weight.

On orchestration & harmony

The orchestration is close to the rest of the show's samba language, but with the piano brought farther forward. That gives the song a more suspended, intimate quality. It still belongs to the world of the ensemble, but it starts from a more interior place.

Harmonically, the song moves between two main sections: a verse and a refrain, each with a different kind of intensity. In the verses, I use a Neapolitan sixth chord as the subdominant harmony, with a sharp 11 on top. That color gives the progression its haunted quality: beautiful, but slightly bent out of shape. The second time we return to the one chord, it turns major, creating a sound related to Hijaz or Phrygian dominant — the pull between the major tonic and the flat-two major chord.

The refrain moves toward the minor four and then circles back to the one, but it does so in a way that feels cyclical rather than conclusive. The harmony keeps turning back on itself, almost like the horizon line. It could keep going around and around forever. The song needs to feel like it is interrupted.

A note from Emily

When Sebastian first described the show's concept and location to me, sunset played a powerful role at the border of theatricality. Like the opening number, this song blends between the world of the play and the world outside it. Under Sebastian's skillful composition, the verses linger, and the choruses roll. Lyrically, the verses take some patience, with an ABCD near-rhyme scheme across stanzas. The chorus bends around into itself.

Song Six

Flowers (How Could You Forget)

Bolero

On content

This song was quick to write. Not because it was simple, but because it made a lot of sense to us. We knew we wanted the song to be about death before we even knew whether there was going to be any death within the story itself.

We wanted to sing about things that a ghost might be upset about someone else forgetting: flowers, candles, and pictures, all of which appear throughout the show as recurring themes. Those objects become altars to the dead. We see that idea referenced again and again throughout the show, and this song brings it forward directly.

On genre

This song steps out of Brazil, but not into a place entirely foreign from Brazil. Here, we explore bolero: a style of music that comes from Cuba and spread across much of Latin America in the mid-20th century. I was inspired by Los Tres Ases and other bolero trio groups from Mexico. It is also a bit of an homage to Tierras Prometidas, a project I was working on when I lived in Mexico City last summer.

Bolero helps us tell this story. The genre is often characterized by sophisticated lyrics, usually having to do with love. Here, we turn that slightly on its head by singing a song from the perspective of the departed, wishing they had been loved more.

On orchestration & harmony

The main thing that had to change was how we played the instruments we already had. We took a pandeiro and turned it into a bongo, playing bolero rhythms on it. We also introduced trumpet, as we did in the previous song, which is a common bolero instrument. The guitar is able to play more floridly here, with extended lines and beautiful harmonies inspired by early bolero trios.

The harmony itself begins with the texture of a line cliché: E minor, with the bass line ascending. Ethan, our guitar player, often commented that it sounded like a grunge song because of the voicings, which leave a lot of open strings ringing. That is also very common in Brazilian music: playing with the sound of open strings as extensions that were not necessarily intentional and could not be created the same way in other keys.

We eventually shift to the major key. But we do not just shift from E minor to G major. We shift to C major, via G7 as a turnaround, which is something I borrowed from Luiz Murá. I think that tells a really interesting story in this melancholy bridge. We are using saudade in a very specific way here: the sorrow of a time forgotten. With the three-part harmony in the bridge, we get a beautiful, rich texture that could only be accomplished with three voices singing together.

A note from Emily

This song has a classic game to it — one idea expressed through different images.

Song Seven

Vai

Samba Pagode

On content

The final song in the show is necessarily a fun one. We wanted to bring back the pagode style from the beginning, except this time with more of a storytelling twist.

We created this character, João, who is represented by something very special in the show, and we wanted to tell his story as a sailor who was always being told to go, go, go. Hence the lyric "vai, vai, vai." When I first wrote the song, I had that chorus in mind before I really knew what the song was about. I knew it had to say "vai, vai, vai," but I did not yet know why.

Sammy, our co-creator, helped us situate that impulse inside the narrative. By the last moments of the play, we get the story of a man who has always been told to go, right as our characters have to decide whether they themselves are going to go or not. That parallel became very powerful. We are saying: go, go, go without fear. Go, go, go with faith.

The final line changes from "go, go, go, boy, go" to "go, go, go, we go," using the phrase a gente. In Brazilian Portuguese, "a gente" literally means "the people," but it is commonly used to mean "we." I love that shift. The song moves from an instruction given to one person into a collective decision. By the end, going is no longer something João is told to do alone. It becomes something everyone chooses together.

On genre

Again, we are in the world of pagode. This time, the tempo is faster, the energy is brighter, and the whole thing is meant to feel like the show throwing its arms open at the end.

We are playing partido alto on the pandeiros, and everyone gets to have a lot of fun with the instruments. We bring in cuíca, agogô, tamborim, rebolo, tantã, and more, which gives the song an exciting lift as it drives toward the final section of the show.

On orchestration & harmony

The harmony is very simple because the song is telling a very simple story. For most of it, we keep it to basically four chords. It has that sunny, open progression that feels right for a final number: immediate, singable, and easy to join.

The bridge gives us a chance to explore slightly new harmonic territory. It connects back a little to the verse of the third song, but it is also inspired by Grupo Fundo de Quintal's beautiful harmonic shifts: I to iii minor to v minor, acting almost like a ii–V–I into the IV, then moving to iv minor and flat VII to bring us back home to the I. That full bridge lets the song open up for a moment before returning to the simple chorus at the end. After all the harmonic turns and story turns, we land back on the thing we knew from the beginning: vai, vai, vai.

A note from Emily

Adventure and structure rule "Vai." The transition of meaning in the chorus lends itself to different setups in the verses. There is an insatiable, driving, spiritual power in the narrative and the music. Inspired by classic myths, it is a song that pits an exploratory character with circular storytelling.
